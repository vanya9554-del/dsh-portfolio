When is the Polymarket crowd wrong?

A calibration study of 1,409 resolved Polymarket markets (2024–2026). For each market I took the Yes price 1, 7, 30, 90 and 180 days before resolution and compared it with the actual outcome, with cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals and 2026 fee/slippage assumptions. Short answer: the crowd is close to right at short horizons; the exploitable mispricing lives at longer horizons and is lottery-shaped.

1. At one day the market is almost perfectly calibrated

Average Yes price 23.94% vs realised frequency 24.65% — a gap of +0.71pp with a confidence interval that crosses zero. Brier skill 0.725, logit slope 1.066 (1.0 = perfectly calibrated). After a one-cent slippage and taker fees, the classic strategies do not survive:

This matches the academic literature: the favourite-longshot bias exists in direction but is smaller than the trading costs. The Princeton calibration thesis on Polymarket reports a logit slope of 1.112, a 2026 Zenodo study about 1.14 — both consistent with a market that is slightly overconfident, not exploitable on its own.

2. The real gap appears at 7–30 days

Seven days before resolution the realised Yes frequency is 24.76% against an average price of 20.62% — an underpricing of +4.14pp (CI +2.43…+5.85, t=4.83). At 30 days the gap is +2.85pp (CI +0.96…+4.84). At one day it disappears. In other words: the crowd updates too slowly as resolution approaches, and cheap Yes contracts are systematically worth slightly more than their price.

The effect is strongest in geopolitics: deadline markets in that category underpriced Yes by +16.35pp at 7 days (n=116). But the distribution is brutal — the biggest misses were Iran peace deal priced 3.9% resolving YES on $177M volume, Ukraine ceasefire priced 6.4% resolving YES on $141M, and “Khamenei out” priced 1.85% resolving YES. Those are lottery tickets: a strategy that buys them needs many independent small bets, not one big one.

3. Copying top traders is survivorship, not skill

I pulled 541 positions from 3,523 buy fills of the top-15 all-time leaderboard. Copying their buys with a one-minute lag gives +13.8% per position in aggregate (t=2.11) — which looks great until you split the wallets: the six high-frequency wallets deliver +6.5% with a confidence interval of −6.1…+19.8 and a 51.9% hit rate, and out-of-sample results range from −14.8% to +73.1%. The aggregate is driven by a handful of huge winners. Classic survivorship bias, not a replicable edge.

4. Biases that did not survive the data

  • Sports draw underpricing: 4 draws in 8 matches priced at 23% — direction right, sample far too small to trade.
  • Home-team bias: not measurable from the public gamma API (no home/away field).
  • News overreaction / mean reversion: 1,498 daily price jumps; only 16.9% mean-reverted, hourly moves mostly continued — no exploitable pattern.

5. What this means in practice

  • There is no easy money for a passive taker. The market is efficient at short horizons and charges enough to erase small biases.
  • The one quantified opportunity is horizon-based: cheap Yes contracts with 7–30 days to go are underpriced on average, especially geopolitical deadline markets. It requires many small, independent positions and tolerance for lottery-like losses.
  • Copy-trading the leaderboard is not a strategy at this sample size: results are dominated by a few wallets and do not hold out of sample.
  • Capital matters: even a real 2% edge is meaningless on $84; this is an evidence question, not an income plan.

Method and limitations

Markets: top-2,100 by volume, resolved between 2024 and 2026 (median volume $9.5M), so the thin long tail is not covered. Prices come from the public CLOB price-history endpoint on an hourly grid; “1 day” is a median of 30 hours before resolution. Confidence intervals are cluster bootstrap. Fees follow the 2026 Polymarket fee formula, slippage assumed at one cent. Read-only: no account, no orders, no KYC, no VPN.
